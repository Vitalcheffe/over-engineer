Essay — April 2026

The Theory of Thresholds

A philosophy of cognitive stages, self-awareness, and human becoming

Amine Harch El Korane — 2026

"What defines a man is not what he achieves — it is where he stops."

I. Introduction — The Inadequacy of a Single Number

For decades, cognitive psychology has attempted to reduce human intelligence to a single figure: the intelligence quotient. This index, introduced by Alfred Binet in 1905 and standardized by Lewis Terman, presents a normal distribution centered on 100 — symmetrical, elegant, and seemingly universal. There are as many individuals at 90 as there are at 110. The curve is perfect. The human being is not.

The IQ measures capacities: working memory, fluid reasoning, verbal processing. But it does not measure what truly distinguishes one life from another. It does not measure the way an individual perceives the world, perceives himself, or decides — consciously or not — to stop. It is precisely this limitation that this framework seeks to move beyond.

What is proposed here is not a refutation of IQ, but its surpassing. An attempt to formalize what the greatest thinkers in history — from Piaget to Kegan, from Pascal to Nietzsche — sensed without articulating in this way: human intelligence is not a score. It is a path. And on that path, everyone stops somewhere.

II. The Structure of Thresholds — A Universal Model

The central hypothesis is as follows: every human being traverses the same stages of cognitive, existential, and identity development. These stages are not tests in any academic sense. They do not announce themselves. They cannot be scheduled. They arise — often quietly — in the way an individual responds to contradiction, endures failure, interprets the success of others, or confronts their own limits.

What we call a "threshold" here is an interior limit. A moment — often imperceptible — where something reconfigures itself in the way of being. Not a piece of knowledge acquired, but a mutation. Those who cross this threshold move to the next level. Those who do not remain — not out of laziness, not out of lack of willpower, but because something, somewhere in their psychic structure, could not transform.

Piaget, in his work on child development, established that cognitive stages follow one another in an invariant order — and that no one can cross a stage without having assimilated the previous one. Vygotsky added that the crossing itself depends on a creative gap between what the individual can do alone and what they can do with guidance — the zone of proximal development. Both theses converge on the same intuition: the passage is structural, not arbitrary.

But where Piaget and Vygotsky stop at childhood, this framework extends the movement across the entirety of adult life. Because the thresholds do not end at adolescence. They continue — subtler, more silent, but just as determining.

III. Robert Kegan and the Stages of Consciousness

Robert Kegan, developmental psychologist at Harvard, dedicated his career to mapping what might be called the stages of adult consciousness. In his foundational work In Over Our Heads (1994), he argues that most adults never reach what he calls the "self-authoring" stage — the level at which the individual stops letting their environment define their values and begins to define themselves.

What Kegan calls a "stage" corresponds exactly to what we call here a threshold. The difference is that Kegan identifies five broad levels, whereas this framework suggests a more granular continuum — micro-thresholds negotiated throughout a lifetime, in domains as diverse as one's relationship to failure, frustration tolerance, and the ability to doubt oneself without dissolving.

"The problem is not that people don't know how to think. It's that they don't know how to see what they cannot yet see."
— Robert Kegan, In Over Our Heads

It is striking that Kegan himself acknowledges that most environments — professional, academic, familial — demand of individuals a level of consciousness they have not yet reached. This permanent gap between what the world requires and what the individual can offer may be the source of most failures that cannot be explained by a lack of intelligence, nor by a lack of effort.

IV. Pascal, Einstein — Where Did They Stop?

The question this framework invites us to ask is not: "Why did they succeed?" It is: "At which threshold did they stop?"

Blaise Pascal was a genius at twelve. His Pensées constitute one of the densest monuments of French philosophical literature. But Pascal stopped. Stopped inside an existential anxiety that even his faith never fully resolved. "The eternal silence of these infinite spaces frightens me." That sentence is not the sentence of a man who has crossed every threshold. It is the sentence of a man who glimpsed one — and could not, or chose not, to cross it.

Albert Einstein revolutionized our conception of space and time. Yet for thirty years he refused to accept quantum mechanics in all its implications. "God does not play dice" — this formula, often admired, is in reality the marker of a man who reached a threshold — that of fundamental randomness — and could not cross it. Not a weakness. A limit. And that is precisely what this framework seeks to name.

No one is exempt. The question is not whether one stops — everyone does — but whether one recognizes the threshold at which one stands.

V. Meta-Consciousness — The Invisible Threshold

Here is the most original point of this framework, and perhaps the most vertiginous: to become aware that a threshold exists is already a threshold.

Metacognition — the capacity to think about one's own thinking — is one of the rarest and most slowly developing faculties in human beings. Flavell, who formalized the concept in the 1970s, demonstrated that the vast majority of individuals never fully activate this capacity. They react. They do not observe themselves reacting.

But this framework goes further. It is not merely a matter of noticing one's own cognitive processes. It is asking, faced with every experience: "Am I crossing a threshold right now? Am I missing one?" This question — posed constantly, as a permanent filter on reality — is itself a threshold that few people ever reach.

"Know thyself."
— Socrates, inscribed at the Temple of Delphi

This Socratic injunction, repeated for twenty-five centuries, is not common sense advice. It is a threshold. The threshold of the one who accepts that their greatest ignorance concerns their own structure. And that threshold — the one of self-awareness as a field of exploration rather than a settled certainty — may be the most determining of all.

VI. Limits, Paradoxes, and Open Questions

Every honest framework must confront its own limits. This one is no exception.

First limit: reversibility. This framework suggests that certain thresholds, once missed, durably structure the individual. But contemporary neuroscience — particularly the work on neural plasticity described by Michael Merzenich — shows that the adult brain remains capable of reconfiguration. Thresholds considered missed can be revisited, sometimes decades later. The model should therefore not be read as a verdict, but as a provisional map.

Second limit: hierarchy. By proposing a sequence of thresholds, this framework risks introducing a hierarchy of human beings. That is not its intention. An individual who stopped at threshold 40 is not inferior to one who reached 80. They are simply different. The path is not a competition. It is a description.

Third limit — and perhaps the deepest: how can one know if they are currently missing a threshold, if the thresholds one misses are precisely those one cannot see? This is the fundamental paradox of all self-analysis: the tool of observation is also the object observed. Wittgenstein put it differently: "The limits of my language are the limits of my world." The limits of my consciousness are the limits of what I can perceive of myself.

VII. Conclusion — The Threshold as a Way of Life

This framework is not a conclusion. It is a tool. A tool for looking differently — not at what someone has accomplished, but at where they stopped. Not at what an individual knows, but at how far they went in transforming themselves.

What is proposed here is an ethics of permanent self-questioning. Every experience — a defeat, an encounter, an idea that unsettles — may be a threshold. The question is not whether one crosses it. It is whether one recognizes it.

Carol Dweck demonstrated that individuals who believe their capacities are modifiable progress further than those who believe them fixed. But this framework goes further: it is not only the belief in modifiability that matters. It is the acuity of the gaze turned on one's own limits. It is the capacity to live in the state of someone who is constantly searching for what they cannot yet see.

"I know one thing — that I know nothing."
— Socrates

That is not modesty. It is the most precise description that exists of someone who has crossed the rarest threshold of all: the one that consists of accepting that the path has no end — and that this is precisely what makes it worth taking.
